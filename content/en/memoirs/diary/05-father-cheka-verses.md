Without you I have no joy;  
No portion and no peace,  
I shall fall into the abyss of night[?],  
If I remain without you.  

Waves beat, spray leaps  
Along the stony shores  
You are not here, my dear one,  
I will give you to no one.  

             T. S.  

Mama often recalled her work  
in the Cheka, her friends.  
She was about 19–20. Young, beautiful,  
a little headstrong.  

Once, having been late for work with a friend,  
they approached their entrance and saw  
a long queue and the commandant  
checking passes. They decided  
not to join the queue, but to come  
later. They went to the square by the Bolshoi  
Theatre and sat on a bench. After sitting  
for quite a while, they went  
to work. The duty officer let them through  
without any trouble, thinking they had been  
out on an errand. When in the department  
they were seen safe and sound, there was  
a great deal of laughter.  

There was a lift in the building, and everyone  
used it, starting with F. Dz. and so on.  
Again late for work, Mama  
flew into the lift, bumping someone  
in the stomach. When she raised her head, she saw  
Dzerzhinsky, began to apologize, and he only

smiled.  

**Episode 3**  

Typists had to take turns  
on duty with the Presidium of the Cheka–OGPU. In 1923,  
during one of the typing shifts there was little  
work, and by the end of the shift — toward midnight —  
she was very tired. Fifteen minutes before  
the end of the typing duty her  
relief arrived. Noticing how she looked,  
the other said:  

— You’re very tired, I can see. I’ll get  
my ration now (a piece of herring, a sweet, and  
a quarter-pound of bread) and start  
work, and you stop typing, rest.  

Mama did just that: she left the typewriter,  
sat in a soft armchair and closed her  
eyes[?], feels that someone is coming toward  
her, and she decides she will not  
type[?].  

— Are you the duty typist? — he asked.  
— Yes, — she answered, without looking at him.  
— I need a document typed urgently.  
— Another typist will come presently; she  
will type it. But he does not leave and  
goes on:  
— I’ve heard that you are a very good typist,  
and I would like you to type it,  
and I would dictate to you.  

Then Mama looked up and saw that  
F. Dz. was standing before her. Greatly embarrassed, she

---

---

<!-- p. 027 -->

quickly stood up and went to the typewriter. F. Dz.  
dictated the text to her, said thank you and left.  
She was very upset and did not apologize for her  
behavior.  

In a dejected state she went home. The  
next day she told about what had happened in  
the department. Everyone sympathized with her, and she expected  
a proper punishment. Days passed, and  
no reprimand was announced. At that time she could  
not understand why her misdeed had been  
forgiven by the Chairman of the Cheka.  

Later she understood: F. Dz. was a warm-hearted,  
sensitive and attentive person. He  
well understood how hard things were  
for young people (and she was then 19).  

**Episode 4.**  

Workers often saw F. Dz. in the departments,  
met him in corridors, on staircases;  
he always looked friendly, neat.  
His kind face and eyes seemed to say:  
“I see it’s hard for you, but what can  
one do? The situation requires it…”  

Mama’s recollections of Dzerzhinsky were printed  
in the collection *F. E. Dzerzhinsky in the Cheka*  
(Library of the magazine *Pogranichnik* No. 14, 1967)

### Clipping (printed text)

**THROUGH THE STORM OF TIME**  
**(FOR THE 100TH ANNIVERSARY OF THE BIRTH OF T. P. SAMSONOV)**

On 19 February 1919 *Pravda* published  
a “Letter to the Editor” in which a group of anarchist-  
communists, responsible workers  
of the staff of the 3rd Army of the Eastern Front,  
openly renouncing the erroneous political  
dogmas that had weighed upon them, expressed their ardent wish  
to join the ranks of the Bolshevik party.  
Assessing this important step, taken  
in the hardest time for the Republic of Soviets  
and becoming a turning point in  
his complex personal fate, one of  
the authors of the letter, our fellow countryman T. P. Sam-  
sonov, late in life wrote: “If one  
transports oneself back to that epoch, it becomes  
perfectly clear that people who lived for the revo-  
lution could not have acted otherwise.”

**[column 1]**  
Timofey Petrovich Samso-  
nov (Babiy) was born on 9 (21)  
May 1888 in a large  
family of a poor peasant of the vil-  
lage of Mikhayleny, now of the Bri-  
chany district. Having early known  
want and hardship, already as a  
primary-school pupil he was  
forced each summer, together with his  
father, to work as a hired hand for  
kulaks and landowners. No eas-  
ier was it for him while  
studying at the Khotyn real  
school. There, as a  
third-year pupil, in the spring  
of 1906 he joined a Social-  
Democratic circle, in  
which under the guidance of  
one of the active participants  
in the December armed  
uprising of 1905 in Moscow,  
who was in hiding in Khotyn,  
he becomes acquainted with Marxist  
literature, takes part in dis-  
tributing among the peasants  
of the surrounding villages leaflets and ap-  
peals.

**[column 2]**  
The first responsible  
assignment that fell  
to the young revolutio-  
nary was participation in the secret  
delivery, from a local private  
printshop, of type for an under-  
ground press set up  
in Kamenets-Podolsk by the gu-  
bernial Social-Democratic  
committee.  

Over the long revolutionary  
activity of T. P. Samsono-  
v he [more than once? / repeatedly?] was subjected to po-  
lice persecution.  

“I sat,” we read  
in one document, “in pri-  
sons from the end of 1907 to 1910.  
In 1910 I again  
go into exile. On the way  
I fall ill with typhus, first in  
Kursk and then in Samara  
prisons. In the prisons, as  
everyone well knows, one  
got it hard. Especially if  
one considers that I was in the Ki-  
ev, Kursk, Tula, Sa-  
mara, Krasnoyarsk, Ir-  
kutsk prisons and in the Alek-  
sandrovsk hard-labor cen-  
tral”

**[column 3]**  
Yet this list  
of prisons is far from complete; it  
was preceded by other pri-  
son casemates…  

Timofey Petro-  
vich was first arrested in September  
1906 and, together with other  
members of the Social-Democratic  
circle, brought to ju-  
dicial responsibility. But,  
released temporarily on  
bail, he at once goes into hiding and  
soon again plunges into the whirl-  
pool of revolutionary  
events. But already under the under-  
ground nickname “Foma” and as  
a member of a group of anarchist-com-  
munists. Inability to wait for  
the class struggle, desperate  
personal courage led to  
Samsonov, following  
the tactics of his group, already in  
early 1907 becoming  
one of the organizers and lead-  
ers of the flying detachment  
of anarchist-communists “Des-  
troy and Create,” which became  
a terror to the propertied classes  
not only in the Khotyn region but  
also in the area of Kamenets-Podol-  
sk.

**[column 1]**  
Later, during one of the party  
purges, he explained these  
actions thus: “The unorganized,  
reckless peasant  
element surrounding me then… pushed me onto  
the path of peasant upris-  
ings, and in essence onto the path  
of despair, the path of petty-bour-  
geois revolutionary spon-  
taneity.” Answering the ques-  
tion what he specifically did  
while an anarchist-com-  
munist, he indicated: “…I took  
part in several expropri-  
ations, prepared and carried  
out arsons of landowners’  
estates, organized pea-  
sant circles of peasants.  
The peasants willingly followed  
me because they knew  
I was of their milieu; they  
trusted me more than the urban  
ones….”  

But the tsarist satraps did not  
sleep either. On 16 December 1907,  
at one of the safe houses in the village  
of Gvozdoutsy of Khotyn uyezd,  
T. Samsonov was seized and  
thrown into Khotyn prison.  
There he first met  
G. I. Kotovsky, whose name as  
a people’s avenger in  
the years of the first Russian revolu-  
tion thundered throughout Bessara-  
bia. A long, friendly conversa-  
tion behind bars with the renow-  
ned Moldavian haiduk etched itself  
for life in the memory  
of the young revolutionary figh-  
ter. Full of optimism and  
hope, Grigory Ivanovich  
told him then of preparations  
for his next escape from  
prison. Timofey was preparing for this  
too. But they did not manage  
to carry out their plans.  

The investigation lasted more than  
a year and a half. At last, by decision  
of the Odessa judicial chamber  
of 28 May 1909, Timo-  
fey Petrovich was sentenced in sum (on  
three cases) to 5  
years’ imprisonment  
and to lifelong exile in Si-  
beria. He served the harsh sentence  
in a number of prisons, and then  
was sent into exile  
in Irkutsk Governorate: first  
to Ichera volost, and from  
1914 to Bodaybo, where  
since the mid-19th century had been  
the main base of the Lena–  
Vitim gold-mining  
region. But even here,  
where the memory of the Lena shooting of 1912  
was still fresh,  
he did not lay down his arms. For  
close ties and anti-state  
activity among  
political hard-labor prisoners Samsonov  
was soon arrested and sentenced  
to 6 months in prison.  

Breaking free, Ti-  
mofey Samsonov escapes  
from hard labor, reaches  
Vladivostok, where he soon se-  
cretly boards the steamer  
*Novgorod* and emigrates to  
England. On arrival in Li-  
verpool he takes a job as a  
dock worker, joins the lo-  
cal organization of anarchist-  
communists and under the name  
Mikhail Nemchinov de-  
velops political,  
mainly anti-war  
activity among Rus-  
sian sailors and English  
dockers.

**[column 2]**  
which especially  
intensifies after the joyful  
news of the victory of the February  
Revolution in Russia, far  
from pleasing to the English  
authorities. In the spring of 1917, during  
a speech at a multi-  
thousand-strong rally against the im-  
perialist war, Ti-  
mofey Petrovich was arres-  
ted and sentenced to 5 months  
hard labor, immediately re-  
placed by compulsory de-  
portation of the defendant to Rus-  
sia.  

Such a turn of events  
only gladdened T. P. Sam-  
sonov, for he longed with all his soul  
for the Motherland, seized  
by the rising revolution-  
ary storm. Arriving on the eve  
of Great October in Arkhan-  
gelsk, Timofey Petrovich  
at once hastens to the Urals, where in  
the October days of 1917  
he takes part in the uprising of the Chelya-  
binsk railwaymen,  
and somewhat later in the sup-  
pression of the counter-revolutio-  
nary mutiny in Irkutsk. As  
a member of the Chelyabinsk Soviet of Wor-  
kers’ and Soldiers’ Depu-  
ties, chairman of the city  
department of the railwaymen’s trade  
union and of the labor exchange, he  
carries out responsible as-  
signments of Tsentrosibir (CEC of the So-  
viets of Siberia).  

Barely returned from the  
2nd All-Russian  
Congress of Labor Commissars held in May 1918  
in Moscow,  
Samsonov was forced  
to take up arms again:  
the outbreak of the Czechoslovak  
mutiny began a new  
stage of the civil war,  
when alongside the expansion  
of its scale the balance  
of forces was on the side  
of internal and external counter-  
revolution. In the hard situ-  
ation that had arisen, Timofey  
Petrovich, true to his re-  
volutionary duty, at once  
forms a fighting workers’ de-  
tachment which, together with oth-  
er units loyal to the Republic of So-  
viets, fights against  
Czechoslovak mutineers  
and SR–White Guard  
detachments. Soon his detachment  
merges into the 3rd Red Ar-  
my of the Eastern Front, which  
in July–August 1918  
conducts stubborn battles on  
the Zlatoust–Chelyabinsk, Ekate-  
rinburg and Perm di-  
rections.  

In the course of these battles the com-  
mand, valuing Samsonov’s  
devotion to the cause of the revolution  
and his readiness for ruthless  
struggle with the enemies, recalls  
Timofey Petrovich to army  
headquarters and appoints him first  
investigator and then se-  
nior instructor of military  
control. The honesty,  
courage and decisiveness natural to him  
prove useful in the new  
post already in the hard  
days when the 3rd Army, after  
stubborn battles and heavy  
losses, was forced  
in December 1918 to leave  
Perm and retreat under the on-  
slaught of Kolchak’s hordes.

**[column 3]**  
The energetic and decisive  
measures taken by Timofe-  
y Petrovich to strengthen  
army units and raise  
their combat readiness were duly  
appreciated by F. E.  
Dzerzhinsky — a member of the spe-  
cial commission of the CC  
of the RCP(b) and the Republic’s Defense  
Council, which arrived by  
order of V. I. Lenin on  
the Eastern Front to investi-  
gate the causes of the defeat  
at Perm. At the same time  
Felix Edmundovich approved  
Samsonov’s proposal to con-  
vert military control into the Special  
Department of the Cheka of the ar-  
my and recommended him for  
the post of head of that  
department. On parting Dzer-  
zhinsky gave Timofey  
Petrovich his personal re-  
volver of the Colt system and  
handed him a certificate for  
the right to carry it.  

The personal meeting with F. E.  
Dzerzhinsky played an impor-  
tant role in the further fate  
of T. P. Samsonov. It was  
precisely it that prompted Timofey Petro-  
vich, as well as his colleagues  
on the army staff, to break in  
February 1919 with anarch-  
ism and join the ranks  
of the RCP(b). In March of the same  
year, by order of Felix Ed-  
mundovich, Samsonov was re-  
called to Moscow and appointed head  
of the Special Department and  
member of the Collegium of the Moscow Cheka, and a  
few months later — head  
of the Military Intelligence  
Directorate of the Main Staff  
of the RKKA. On the recommendation  
of the Chairman of the Cheka, Timofey  
Petrovich in 1920 be-  
comes Administrator and  
head of the Organizational-  
Instructor Department  
of Glavkomtrud of the RSFSR, whose  
chairman concurrently  
was F. E. Dzer-  
zhinsky.  

The seething energy of T. P. Sam-  
sonov, his many-sided acti-  
vity devoted to the cause  
of the party and the strengthening of the So-  
viet state, especially  
vividly showed itself in 1921–1924,  
when he headed  
the Special Department and was a member of  
the Collegium of the Cheka–OGPU. With his  
name is linked the enormous work  
of Soviet chekists in  
uncovering and neutralizing  
a whole series of major  
counter-revolutionary conspir-  
acies. Here is the suppression  
of the Kronstadt mutiny,  
which broke out inspired  
by the RCP(b)[? — in the text, proba-  
bly “by SRs/the Entente”; the bottom  
of the column is damaged], and also the [Kho-  
lostoy?] Antonov mutiny in the Tambov  
region. Here is the uncovering of the ca-  
det conspiracy headed  
by former minister of the Provi-  
sional Government Kish-  
kin. Here is the skillfully car-  
ried-out operation of the arrest in  
Kiev of the last under-  
ground congress of the party of Right SRs…  
Many years will pass,  
and abroad a whole  
series of books will appear in which were…  
shamelessly, falsely, and  
other unfinished deadly  
enemies of the Land of Soviets  
will tearfully recount how  
under the leadership of “Iron  
Felix” Timofey  
Petrovich dealt one after  
another inimitable blows  
to the supporters of strangling So-  
viet [power…]

### Printed text (continuation of the article, 2 columns)

**[column 1]**  
His contribution to  
the struggle against counter-revolution, to  
the cause of strengthening the organs of state  
security  
was duly noted: in  
1921 he was awarded  
the Order of the Red Banner, and  
somewhat later he was  
granted the title of Honorary  
Chekist and issued a certifi-  
cate over the personal signature of F. E.  
Dzerzhinsky.  

Yet the most respon-  
sible and at the same time most  
honorable assignment that  
T. P. Samsonov had to car-  
ry out as head of  
the Special Department of the Cheka was,  
as he himself considered, ensur-  
ing the security of V. I. Lenin in  
early 1922. As is known,  
by decision of the Party CC of  
31 December 1921 Vladi-  
mir Ilyich was granted  
a six-week leave  
for treatment and rest. The first  
week he lived at Gorki,  
but since the whole territory  
there was open and it was  
unsafe for Lenin to live, on  
assignment from F. E. Dzerzhins-  
ky Timofey Petrovich after  
several days of searching chose  
a new place — a small  
house near the village of Ko-  
stino, where V. I. Lenin together  
with N. K. Krupskaya and M. I.  
Ulyanova lived from 17 Jan-  
uary to 1 March 1922.  
By order of the Chairman of the Cheka  
full responsibility for  
the security of Vladimir Ilyich for  
all that time was laid  
on Samsonov.

**[column 2]**  
Wherever the Party CC later  
sent him,  
T. P. Samsonov every-  
where justified the high trust of the par-  
ty: as head of  
a Directorate of the VSNKh, as Ad-  
ministrator of the CC of the VKP(b),  
and then of the Executive Committee of the Comin-  
tern, and in subsequent high  
and responsible posts  
connected with the development  
of the country’s economy, science and culture.  
Evidence of his  
maturity and of the trust in him  
is that T. P.  
Samsonov was elected a delegate  
to the 16th and 17th Party Congresses,  
to a number of all-union party  
conferences, and twice was  
a deputy of the Moscow Soviet.  

Already on well-earned  
retirement, T. P. Samsonov con-  
tinued to engage actively in  
public activity.  
Resuming work on his  
recollections begun back in  
the 1930s, he labored  
persistently on the manuscript  
*Notes of a Chekist*. But  
he was not fated to finish this work:  
on 28 October 1955  
the faithful son of Lenin’s party  
and ardent patriot  
of the Soviet Motherland was no more.  

The working people of Moldavia, like  
all Soviet people, highly  
value the glorious deeds of their  
fellow countryman. On the house in which  
T. P. Samsonov was born a me-  
morial plaque has been installed,  
and the Mikhayleny secondary school  
has been given his name.  

**I. BOBEYKO,**  
senior research asso-  
ciate of the Institute of Party  
History under the CC of the CP of Molda-  
via, Candidate of Histori-  
cal Sciences.

Verses about Papa written by my sister Gena

Do you remember, Papa, the quiet evening,  
Stars and the moon,  
We sat together in the hammock —  
Autumn already was.

You spoke verses like a song,  
Helped us.  
Papa, do you remember that evening —  
We were together.

And then, who knew what would be,  
A year passed, night followed day,  
But your dear close image  
Your daughter will not forget.

The heart beats, the eyes flow,  
For this is life!  
Daughters leave their fathers,  
It forever flows into the past.

But those good years —  
Can they be forgotten?  
I wished, Papa, (that)  
They could forever return

Do you remember, Papa, how you sang to us  
Your storm and thunder

“The wide Dnieper roars and groans.”  
We still ask afterward!

Tell us, dear Papa,  
How in one village  
There lived “Koblan[?] the dog,”

And till late at night you spend evenings  
Our native home

Where are your games, jokes,  
Mischief sometimes,

The joy of past days.

My dear and beloved,  
My dear father,  
I love you, I kiss you,

Gena
