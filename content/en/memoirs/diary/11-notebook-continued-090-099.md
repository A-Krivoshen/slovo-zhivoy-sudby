*From the handwritten notebook “Our genealogy: the Samsonovs.”*

*On p. 093 the brother is named Timofey Timofeevich (Tyoma); later the manuscript often calls him Zhenya.*

And she invited Zhenya to stay with her. Zhenya left.  
Mama was very worried, but we did not  
notice. Mama had a strong character.  
Mama went to a well-known Moscow  
doctor about the fact that Zhenya was going to marry.  
Zhenya had met a young lieutenant there  
and apparently fallen in love. The doctor said to Mama:  
“Well, what can one do? Only she must not  
have children.”

Sometime in the autumn of ’39 or ’40 Zhenya  
came to us with her husband and  
she was already expecting a child. For her health  
that was no good, and at that time  
her [mother? / husband?] leaves for Magadan and leaves  
her alone with a little child (a boy was born,  
they named him Kima[?]); it was already beyond  
her strength and her health began  
to worsen. She came to us in  
May 1941 with her son. Of course we  
did everything to surround[?] her; Grandmother  
took the child so that Zhenya could sleep  
at night. Mama went to the doctors,  
but her health grew much worse. They  
treated her, but June passed and on 22 June  
the war began. Evacuation began, and  
our parents decided to send us to  
Kuibyshev, where her husband worked.  
And so we set off: Zhenya, her son,  
Grandmother and I. Mama took us.  
Mama stayed with us for a while,  
tried to settle us. They found  
for Zhenya, her husband, and us 2 rooms in a military  
hostel. Mama found a woman  
who would look after the boy.  
Zhenya was put in hospital. I was 14,  
and Grandmother 77–78. [At first Mama still  
managed?] to leave, and we remained  
with Grandmother and the little child.  
[Nikolai?] worked a great deal. Zhenya — in hospital.  
At the beginning of September the boy fell ill  
with dysentery; he was put in hospital,  
and from there he did not return. He was  
10 months old. Zhenya did not tell us  
of this loss. She was [quiet?]. I vis-  
ited her in hospital, but she was weak.  
In the month of October she was gone. She was 21.  
Such was the fate of my sister. When  
she came to us, she said  
to Mama: “You are my mama”[?]. I am very sorry  
she is gone. She was always  
needed by me, though in youth I [somewhat?]  
understood that. I am very sorry she is gone.  
She was kind, good, talented,  
sang well. We were very  
alike. Once, standing before the mirror together,  
she said: “Here is sister[?] Samsonova[?].”

**My brother.**

Caption by the portrait (see figure above): *After the war.*

My brother, Samsonov Timofey  
Timofeevich, was born in the city of Moscow  
on 27 April 1923.

There were three of us and in childhood we were all  
very close.  
We played together; if it hap-  
pened we fought, then again  
made friends, quarrelled over  
the cat (with whom she would sleep).  
Once, for our peace of  
mind, our parents got us  
each a kitten so that no one would  
feel left out.  
When we played at home,  
Zhenya always pretended to be a  
dog that guarded  
our house. For that a kennel was  
built; Tyoma crawled under  
the table, some cover was thrown  
over the table, and there lived our “dog.”  
Once while playing we invented a house for ourselves.  
These were wardrobes; one small wardrobe  
where our books were served as a house  
for me. Zhenya chose our clothes  
wardrobe. This was a town, and Tyoma was the chief  
of the guard-post; he had the keys to the wardrobes.  
By “day” he let us out, and by “night” locked us in.  
It happened that he could not open  
the wardrobe in which I sat. I was very fright-  
ened that I would not get out and began  
to cry, saying: “I’m stifling, I  
haven’t enough air!” Zhenya took fright and  
rushed to the kitchen to Grandmother, shouting:  
“Tanya is suffocating.” Grandmother came and  
opened the wardrobe. I came out free.

Tyoma went to school while we still  
lived on Mokhovaya. The school was  
on Frunze Street (Znamenka) and they walked him  
to school. The building of that school is old;  
it still stands. Now the  
Central Children’s Music School is there.  
When Natasha and I came (Natasha —  
our house worker), the silence astonished me  
(lessons were on), and I was afraid to cough.  
Then the move to Serafimovich Street and  
Tyoma began to attend the Menzhinsky  
school (somewhere in the centre). Zhenya  
studied there too.

At the dacha we spent most of our time  
on the river. When they built  
the canal and the Moskva River grew deep,  
the station “Okrug” opened near us.

{{< figure src="/photos/dnevnik-tt/str-094.jpg" caption="Brother — dacha, school, friends." >}}

(who had saved [him?] from the water). Zhenya spends  
all his days there, swimming[?], [on duty?], and they  
[?] in a boat and watched the bath-  
ers. Once those on duty treat-  
ed him to water (and he was about 14).  
Our parents were upset, but did not scold him.  
Father spoke with him seriously, and then  
often teased him. But that hap-  
pened only once.

It must be said that in our house wine  
was not held in honour. We never saw  
our parents drink wine in our  
presence. We never even saw  
in the “underground” [vessels?]. [It?] [was?]  
allowed[?] to stand in our sideboard, [suddenly?]  
even a little carafe at times. Sometimes after  
the cold Papa allowed himself one  
glass.

In summer Zhenya always built huts,  
played the piano well, showed  
us various tricks. In the evening we  
loved very much to play volleyball and  
played well, properly.

When I went to school I was as-  
signed to school No. 13 not far from home,  
on Sofiyskaya Embankment. And we began  
to see less of Zhenya, because I studied  
in the 1st shift and Zhenya in the 2nd. Then  
Mama transferred him to a school closer  
to her work, so as to be able  
to keep an eye on him. Mama then worked  
in the Railway district Party committee, on Krasnopres-  
nenskaya, and Zhenya went there every day.

A little later Zhenya became interested  
in photography, and in our album there are  
photographs taken by him. He played  
chess well!

Then new friends appeared for him and  
for me. His best friends were Lyova  
Uralov (or Leva); [he called Zhenya  
Misha?]. Lyova lived in our entrance  
on the 8th floor. They were very close.  
Unfortunately Lyova was killed in the war,  
at the front.

And for the time being they spent time together,  
[skipped?] lessons together. And they did it  
like this: Lyova studied in the 1st shift, and Zhenya  
in the 2nd. They met between floors  
in the entrance and spent time together  
in the yard!  
They were very good[?] little sailors[?], because

<!-- p. 095 -->

{{< figure src="/photos/dnevnik-tt/str-095.jpg" caption="1941; the front; return from the war." >}}

Lyova had an uncle who was a sailor. The boys flared[?]  
their trousers and put in gores so that  
they got bell-bottoms. They were very proud  
of those bell-bottoms. And there were no striped singlets.  
Then they took paper and drew  
stripes like a singlet. Then they put that  
paper under the shirt so that it would be  
seen.

They were friends with our friends[?]: Ilyusha  
and Kolya Strelkov. So it drew[?] them [closer?]  
until June 1941.  
Zhenya was finishing the ninth form, and Lyova —  
either the 8th or the 9th, I don’t remember exactly.

And then came 22 June 1941. Every-  
thing changed. In July we left for  
evacuation, and Papa, Mama and Zhenya  
stayed in Moscow. Papa [went?] to work  
at the “Krasny Fakel” plant, which  
was on Sofiyskaya Embankment. Now  
it is gone. Torn down. And during  
air-raid alerts they stood watch on the roof  
of the “Udarnik,” throwing down incendiary  
bombs.

16 October 1941. Panic in Moscow.  
As I already wrote, Mama worked  
in the Railway district Party committee. It was de-  
cided to take the Party activists out of Moscow.  
They called a car from the district committee; it  
went round the workers’ addresses, gath-  
ering them, and then brought them to  
the square in front of Kazansky Station.  
And then they were loaded into wagons  
and sent to Tambov region.  
Mama left Moscow with Tasya  
and Dusya (our house worker), and Papa  
stayed in Moscow.  
They arrived in the district centre Morshansk  
of Tambov region, 200 km from  
Tambov. From there Zhenya was called  
into the army. First he trained for a time  
in Sverdlovsk, and from there — to  
the front. He described his front-line days  
in detail in his book “The 49th  
Guards Mortar [Regiment].” He went from  
Stalingrad to Berlin. He was wounded,  
shell-shocked, but always returned to  
his regiment. And after the battle of Stalin-  
grad (20% of their regiment remained)  
they were sent to Moscow for re-forming.  
It was so unexpected; we were all  
happy to see him on the threshold  
of the house. He was filthy-filthy; there were  
lice [in the bathhouse?]. They put him straight  
in the bath (Good that we had hot  
water), [rubbed?] him with linen. They took him into

went. But at home he was not very [well], and then  
in January he got up from his bed. His rela-  
tives came to him. We always met  
them with joy. Mama, like any other  
mother, wanted to do something for her son,  
to ease his lot somehow. We  
knew a lieutenant-general, Pavel  
Aleksandrovich. And Mama wanted to place  
her brother with him. But Zhenya  
refused, saying he did not want to be  
[a staff officer?].

Zhenya always was and is a very  
honest, principled, decent  
man.

Soon they were sent somewhere [again?]  
and we did not see him again until the end  
of the war. He served on the “Katyushas.”

During his stay in Moscow  
he met his best  
childhood friend — Lyova. Lyova was still  
in Moscow. Then he [everything?], he went  
into a military school, and from there he  
left for the front. Soon he was killed  
in battle.

And for Zhenya what followed was: the battle  
of Kursk, the crossing of the Oder and the tak-  
ing of Berlin. He met the end of the war in  
Berlin. They did not release him home at once;  
he still served some time more.  
He came home in the spring of 1947.  
In Berlin he married Klavdia  
Georgievna Vsevolodova (Sima). She too  
had gone through the war, but in the 3rd echelon.  
She was an Accountant. She was from Voronezh.

Zhenya always brought his fellow  
soldiers to us. I remember in the year ’43 or ’44  
a comrade of his lived with us. He had come  
from hospital; he had lost a leg.  
He was from Ukraine, from the city of Sumy.  
Soon he went home.

In the spring of ’45 two [cripples?] came,  
Zhenya’s boys from his “Katyusha.”  
One could understand them. They had gone through  
the whole war and returned as victors.  
They too lived with us for a time,  
and then — home.

At the end of the war soldiers from the front  
sent home parcels with various  
things. Our Zhenya did not send  
a single one. He said: “We need nothing.  
We have everything.” Such is his character.

<!-- p. 097 -->

{{< figure src="/photos/dnevnik-tt/str-097.jpg" caption="After the war — Vorobyovo, books, retirement." >}}

After his return he went to study at  
a school of surveyors and became a surveyor.  
At that time I had already finished the 10th form and was living  
with Sima. For a time they lived with  
Mama, and then in 1949 they left for Vorobyovo,  
where Sima had her mother and sister.  
That is how Zhenya ended up in Vorobyovo.  
There he studied at an evening institute, but  
in my view he did not finish it. They had  
no children, because Sima was ill  
with tuberculosis. The doctors did not recom-  
mend that she have children. For a long time Zhenya  
worked at some institute (trust), where  
he was head of the [publications?] department.  
He travelled all over Central Russia.  
There is no family any more. Zhenya — has none.  
He comes to us, visits us.  
He published two books about Father and  
about his [folder? / regiment?]. The first — “A Rank-and-File Revo-  
lutionary,” dedicated “to the bright memory  
of Timofey Petrovich Samsonov,” and  
the second — “The 49th Guards Mortar [Regiment].”

I repeat again: “Zhenya was and is  
a very decent, honest, principled  
man. To the end of his life  
he will remain a Communist. He is a very  
responsive person, always ready  
to help anyone who needed  
it. At work he enjoyed  
the authority and love of his colleagues.”

He is a very keen man. He has  
good hands. In retirement he  
took up the history of Vorobyovo as well;  
he speaks in schools, where he tells  
the children about the war, about the fighting  
near Voronezh. He also spoke  
on local television on the  
same subject. He is a member of the Committee of War  
Veterans, where he works very actively.  
And he is also on very friendly terms with the town  
library; they gave him a room  
where he arranges various exhibitions.  
For example: “VO-series of the Russian fleet”  
(for our fleet was born in Voronezh).  
He is always busy with some  
affair or other. In the Library he “treats” books.  
He learned bookbinding and  
binds books that need  
“repair.”
