**Mother**

My mother is Samsonova Evdokia (Eva — that is what  
her father and husband called her) (Morozova)  
Konstantinovna.

**[photo]** Two portraits of Mama: in glasses, dark clothes; an earlier lighter portrait.

She was born on 14 March (2 March by the old  
style) 1900 in Moscow.  
Her father, Morozov Konstantin Mikhailovich,  
came from the village of Griboevo, Kaievskaya[?] volost,  
Klin uyezd of Moscow guberniya. A worker.  
He looked after the house of the civilian general  
Tikhanov[? / Chikalyov?]. He was an electrician, a plumber, and watched  
over the condition of the house. In that house lived  
my grandmother, my grandfather, and Mama. They had  
2 rooms on the 1st floor; the windows  
looked out onto the courtyard. That house stood  
on 2nd Brestskaya Street not far from  
Belorussky Station. That five-storey  
house still stands today.

Her mother was Solovyova Vera Osipovna.  
As a thirteen-year-old girl she was brought to  
Moscow and placed as a nanny in a German  
family whose head was the director of  
breweries. The masters treated  
my grandmother fairly well; they taught her  
neatness, cleanliness, and order. The mistress  
herself plaited her braid (as “her own German  
girl”). Not all of those German qualities  
stayed with Grandmother.

When the masters prepared to leave for Germany,  
they asked Grandmother to come with them. But Grandmother  
was afraid to go to a foreign land and stayed  
in Moscow. Soon she met her future  
[husband?]. Grandmother herself was from Ryazan guberniya,  
the village of Bogoslovo (now Poshunovo[?]), which stands  
on the bank of the Oka. The village stands on  
a high bank. Passing by steamer  
along the Oka past the village one can see  
the tall belfry of the monastery church,  
the monastery wood, where there is a holy  
spring.

Mama was the only child in  
the family (her sister died early). In the family  
they called Mama Dima. At first she  
studied at a city school where at recess,  
in Mama’s words, they gave a mug of  
milk and bread. Then she was placed  
in the Ksenia School for girls. (The  
general — the master of the house — helped with this.  
He was a member of the Board of Trustees.)  
The full name of the school was the School  
named after Ksenia, and the headmistress was Princess  
Drutskaya-Sokolinskaya[?]. At that school  
they trained home teachers.  
It was located in Khamovniki. The girls  
lived there all week, and on Sundays  
they were let home. Rise was at 7  
in the morning; the girls made their beds; for this

a class mistress watched. Then they went to the wash-  
room, where the girls washed to the  
waist in cold water (there was no hot water),  
combed their hair, cleaned their shoes. At the exit  
stood the class mistress and inspected  
each girl. If something was not  
right, she was sent back to put the  
fault right. Then there was a prayer and they went  
to the dining room for breakfast. They had free time,  
and often they walked in the park. Mama  
told many interesting things about her school.  
1917. Mama finished school and pinned  
a red bow to her coat.  
Even in childhood Mama dreamed of becoming  
a typist, and passing by a pharmacy  
she would stand for a long time at the window watching  
the typist who was typing  
prescriptions. Mama went to typing  
courses and became a typist.  
In 1918 her father dies of the Spanish flu (influenza).  
He had gone to the countryside for food and fell ill.  
At that time Moscow was hungry and  
cold. He was buried at the cemetery of the Strastnoy  
Monastery; in its place now stands the “Rossiya” cinema.  
Grandmother did not work.  
Having finished the courses, Mama took a job  
with an organization called  
“Tsentropechat”[?], and from there she was taken  
to work at the Cheka as a typist in  
the secret department.

<!-- p. 010 -->

Mama said she was very afraid to go  
to work, because she was afraid she would spend all her money  
on chocolate. She loved chocolate very much.  
But the times were hard: there was not enough bread;  
they made cutlets from potato peelings.  
The winter was cold. The trams did not run;  
Mama ran to work from Belorussky  
Station to Lubyanka. Sometimes at work  
they were given frozen potatoes, and Mama  
hauled a sack of those potatoes on a sled  
to Belorussky Station. They worked late,  
so she had to drag those sleds through  
dark Moscow.

Mama told many interesting things about  
old Moscow. “The horse-drawn tram ran along the Garden  
Ring, and when it approached Mayakovsky  
Square the conductor would shout: ‘Trukhmany  
(instead of Triumphal) Gates.’  
‘At 6 in the evening Moscow is quieter than water,  
lower than grass.’ Mama was sent to Tverskaya  
for sweet fruit, and also Grandmother’s —  
a kalach. A samovar stood on the table; over  
the table hung a lamp (still kerosene);  
Grandfather put on his glasses and began to read  
aloud the newspaper *Moskovskie Novosti*.

So Mama works at the Cheka, where she  
met her destiny: Samsonov Timofey  
Petrovich, head of the secret department.
